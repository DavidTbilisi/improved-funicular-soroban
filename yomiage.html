Pick a rung and the caller opens with “Ready” — that is your cue, and the board is cleared for you on it. Then each number is spoken as a number (“three hundred twenty-five”, never “three two five”): set it on the beads as you hear it and let the board carry the running total, because holding the numbers in your head instead is a different exercise, and a harder one you have not earned yet. At the end you are asked for the total; hand in what the board reads.
There is no replay. A caller does not repeat a number, so a term you miss is gone — keep the rods you have rather than stopping to recover it. The gap between calls is the difficulty here, not how fast the voice talks: it is how long you have to hear a number, set it, and be ready for the next. Speed changes apply to the next round, never to a round already being called.

Where you are

A rung is carried when you take its whole streak unbroken, and the pace you did it at is banked as the record for that shape. One lucky round at a short gap is not a record.

Why an ear exercise

Every other page here reaches the rods through the eyes: guided practice shows you a problem, flash anzan shows it faster, the kyu paper prints a column. 読上算 is the third modality, and in a real soroban school it is graded on its own certificate — because hearing “minus sixty-three” and having the borrow happen is not the same skill as reading it.

It is also the one exercise where the board is doing the remembering. That is why it is worth doing early: the column can be ten terms long and three digits wide without ever taxing your memory, so what gets trained is purely the path from the sound to the fingers.

The column itself is the kyu paper's 見取算 column — the same generator, so the same rules hold: every number is exactly the rung's width, and a minus term never takes the running total below zero, because there is nothing to the left of the top rod to borrow from.
